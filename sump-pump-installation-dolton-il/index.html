Sump Pump Installation in Dolton, IL

A sump pump collects groundwater in a pit and pushes it outside. Installation means digging the pit, setting the pump, running a discharge line and planning for storms and outages.

A sump pump is a small pump that sits in a pit in the lowest part of your basement or crawlspace. When groundwater fills the pit, a float switch turns the pump on and sends the water out through a discharge pipe. Sump pump installation in Dolton, IL has five parts: the pit, the pump, a check valve, a discharge line and a power plan.

This guide covers each part and the choices that matter. It also says what a sump pump does not do, because it does not stop sewer backup or fix a floor drain that overflows.

Why Dolton basements ask for a pump

Cook County's hazard mitigation plan for Dolton describes urban and repetitive basement flooding across entire neighborhoods, naming the Southern Corridor, the Cornell area and 158th and Greenwood. FEMA counts 34 repetitive-loss properties in the village. The plan also notes that annual precipitation averages about 36 inches, often in short, hard storms.

A pump helps with groundwater. It does not help when the sewer backs up through a floor drain, which needs a different fix. The Illinois Department of Insurance says standard flood coverage does not typically cover sump pump failure or sewer backup, so ask your agent about adding it. The department's hotline is 1-866-445-5364.

Sources: Cook County hazard mitigation plan, Dolton annex, 2024. Illinois Department of Insurance.

How the install goes, start to finish

A typical installation takes most of a day. Here is the order we follow.

1

Locate the pit and cut the slab

We pick the lowest spot and the shortest path to an outside wall. A concrete saw or jackhammer opens a hole about 18 inches wide, and we dig the pit about 24 inches deep.

2

Set the liner and the pump

A perforated liner goes in and gravel is packed around it. The pump sits on the bottom, level, so the float has room to move.

3

Connect the check valve and discharge

A check valve on the discharge stops water from falling back into the pit. The pipe runs through the rim joist to a discharge point away from the house.

4

Power, test and seal

The pump plugs into a grounded, protected outlet. We fill the pit with water, check the float cycle, then patch the slab around the lid.

Submersible or pedestal pump

Both move water. They differ in cost, noise and how they handle a full pit.

Submersible

  • Sits in the water, so it is quiet
  • Handles heavy flow well
  • Hard to service because it is under water
  • Common in finished basements

Pedestal

  • Motor sits above the pit
  • Easier to reach and often cheaper
  • Louder and sits in the way
  • Best for small pits and light use

Which one fits depends on the pit and the usage. Our sump pump page covers replacement and repair.

Power outages and storm planning

The hazard plan lists power outages among storm impacts, and nighttime thunderstorms are common in the area. A pump that depends on household power stops when the power does. A battery backup pump, set slightly higher in the pit, can keep running for several hours depending on the battery and the flow.

Test the pump twice a year by pouring a bucket of water into the pit. Listen for the pump to start and shut off, and check that water leaves the discharge outside. Keep the pit lid on and the area clear.

Discharge lines, digging and local rules

The discharge pipe should empty outside, well away from the foundation. Do not send it into the sanitary sewer, and ask the Dolton building department, Housing and Permits, at 708-201-3261 what rules apply, because permit rules vary. A pipe that dumps next to the house can send the water right back into the pit.

If the discharge line needs a trench, call JULIE at 811 at least 48 hours before digging so utilities are marked. It is free. Winter matters too, so ask about a freeze-resistant outlet that will not ice over.

What a sump pump will not fix

A pump will not stop a floor drain from backing up when the sewer fills. It will not seal foundation cracks or stop water coming through a wall. It will not help during a power outage unless there is a backup.

For seepage, cracks and wall moisture, a waterproofing system is a separate answer. See basement waterproofing for how drain tile and a sump work together. If a backup is the issue, ask about an overhead sewer or backwater valve, which is plumbing work and not a pump.
