A disposal is a small motor driving a spinning plate inside a grinding chamber. It does not have blades like a blender, and it cannot handle everything. Most calls come down to a jam, a worn seal, or a drain that is blocked beyond the unit.

Disposal jammed or leaking: what to do first

Safety comes before the fix. A disposal that looks dead can start spinning the moment power returns.

Cut the power

Switch it off at the wall, then unplug it under the sink or turn off its breaker. A plain switch is not enough if a hand might go near the opening.

Look, do not reach

Shine a flashlight into the opening. If you see a spoon, bottle cap or bone, lift it out with long tongs or needle-nose pliers.

Free the plate from below

Many units have a 1/4 inch hex socket on the bottom center. Turn a hex wrench back and forth until the plate moves freely, then press the red reset button.

Stop using the sink if it leaks

Put a bucket under the unit and close both stops under the sink. Do not run the dishwasher until we check the drain hose.

Do not use chemical cleaner

Caustic liquid in a stalled unit splashes back and attacks seals. It does nothing for a mechanical jam.

What must never go down a disposal

Most jams and clogs are the result of a short list of foods. The list is the same for every brand.

  • Grease, oil and fat, which cool and coat the drain pipe after the unit
  • Stringy vegetables such as celery stalks, corn husks and onion skins
  • Rice, pasta and oatmeal, which swell into paste
  • Large bones, fruit pits and nut shells
  • Coffee grounds in volume, since they pack into a dense layer
  • Glass, metal, plastic and bottle caps

Your dishwasher drain runs through the disposal

The dishwasher drain hose connects to a small spout on the side of the disposal. If dishwasher water backs up into the sink, or the dishwasher stops draining, the problem may be at that spout rather than in the machine.

A new disposal ships with a plastic knockout plug blocking that spout. If it is not removed during installation, the dishwasher cannot drain at all. We check this on every install, and we confirm the hose forms a high loop so sink water cannot siphon backward.

When water backs up in the sink and the unit is clear, the blockage sits further down the line. That is a job for our drain cleaning crew, and we can do it in the same visit.

Repair it or buy a new one?

A jam caught early often clears with one hand tool. Other failures decide the question for you.

Repair is reasonable

  • The plate turns after a hex wrench reset and the unit runs cleanly
  • The leak is at the top seal or the dishwasher hose
  • The motor is quiet and the housing is dry
  • A reset button trips only once in a while

Replace is the better bet

  • Water seeps through the bottom or the side of the housing
  • It jams again within days of a reset
  • The housing is rusted or the motor is loud and weak
  • The unit is undersized for the household

If the sink itself is also being replaced, our plumbing installation work lets us set both in one visit.

A swap takes planning around power, space and the drain connection.

3

Mount and align

The new unit hangs from the sink flange, the trap is reconnected, and the dishwasher hose goes on after the knockout is cleared.

4

Power up and test

We run water, test the dishwasher drain, check every joint for drips, and show you the reset button and the hex socket.

Electrical and manufacturer guidelines for disposals

A disposal is a wired appliance, so electrical rules apply alongside plumbing ones. Electrical work follows the National Electrical Code (NFPA 70) as adopted locally, and a brand-new circuit is electrician work. Swapping a unit onto an existing plug or hardwire connection is routine.

The most useful guideline on any disposal is the manufacturer's own sheet, which names the reset button, the hex socket size and the fastening style for that model. Keep it, or look the model up before you start.

Sources: National Electrical Code (NFPA 70), manufacturer instructions.
