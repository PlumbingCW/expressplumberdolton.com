How to Replace a Toilet Flapper in Dolton, IL

The flapper is the rubber seal at the bottom of your toilet tank. When it stops sealing, the toilet runs. You can swap it in about fifteen minutes with no tools.

To install a flapper in a toilet tank, you shut the water off, drain the tank, unhook the old flapper from its two ears at the base of the overflow tube, hook the new one on, and set the chain. That is the whole job in Dolton, IL or anywhere else. The tricky part is buying the right flapper and knowing whether the flapper was even the problem.

A flapper lifts when you flush, lets water drop into the bowl, then falls back and seals the opening. If it leaks, the tank slowly empties and the fill valve keeps topping it up. That is the sound of a toilet running at 2 a.m.

Confirm the flapper is the culprit first

Before you spend money, run two quick checks. They take five minutes and save a wasted trip to the hardware store.

  • Drop a few drops of food coloring in the tank and wait 15 minutes without flushing
  • If color shows in the bowl, the flapper or its seat is leaking
  • Press down on the flapper by hand and listen for the running to stop
  • Look at the water level, which should sit about an inch below the top of the overflow tube
  • Check the chain for slack that gets caught under the flapper

Two inch or three inch flapper

Flappers are not universal. The drain opening in the tank bottom comes in two common sizes, and a mismatch will never seal.

Two inch flush valve

  • Found on most older toilets
  • Opening measures about 2 inches across
  • Flapper has a smaller rubber disc
  • Uses less water per flush as a rule

Three inch flush valve

  • Common on many newer and high-performance toilets
  • Opening measures about 3 inches across
  • Flapper has a wider rubber disc
  • Drops water faster for a stronger flush

Measure the opening before you shop, or take the old flapper with you. Some toilets use a flapperless canister or a tower flush valve, and those need a different part. If yours is one of them, call for toilet repair rather than guessing.

Install the new flapper in four steps

Lay a towel on the floor and keep a bucket nearby. You do not need tools.

1

Shut the water and drain the tank

Turn the oval handle on the supply line, near the floor behind the toilet, clockwise until it stops. Flush once to empty the tank.

2

Remove the old flapper

Unclip the chain from the flush lever arm. Slide each side of the flapper off the small pegs on the overflow tube. Some flappers have a ring that slips around the tube instead.

3

Wipe the seat and fit the new one

Rub the plastic seat with a damp cloth to remove slime and mineral film. Attach the new flapper to the pegs and make sure it drops flat over the opening.

4

Set the chain and test

Clip the chain so there is about half an inch of slack when the flapper is closed. Open the supply, let the tank fill, flush a few times and watch for leaks.

Why rubber parts wear in Dolton tanks

The Village of Dolton buys treated Lake Michigan water from the City of Chicago, and the 2025 Consumer Confidence Report lists a chlorine range of 1.0 to 1.4 ppm. Chlorine keeps drinking water safe to move through pipes, and it also slowly stiffens and warps rubber that sits in it for years.

That is why a flapper that looks fine can stop sealing. If the old one feels slick, curled, sticky or hard, expect to replace it every few years. The report does not give hardness, so test your tap with a kit before blaming mineral buildup. For bill questions, ask the Dolton Water Department at 708-201-3293.

Chain length is where most DIY flapper jobs go wrong

A chain that is too tight holds the flapper slightly open, so the toilet runs forever. A chain that is too loose gets under the flapper and keeps it from closing. Aim for about half an inch of slack, roughly the width of a pencil, when the tank is full.

Cut off any extra chain, or tuck it so it cannot fall under the flapper. If you flush and the flapper closes too quickly, your toilet may be short on flush volume. Look for a chain that attaches higher on the lever arm, or try a slightly longer one.

What a new flapper will not fix

A flapper only controls the flow from the tank to the bowl. It will not fix a clogged toilet, a weak flush from blocked rim jets, a rocking bowl or a leak at the floor. A cracked tank or bowl cannot be repaired with any part from the aisle.

If the water level never stops rising in the bowl, close the supply valve and call. If a worn shut-off valve will not turn, do not force it, because the stem can snap and flood the room. That is a small job for a plumber, and we also handle plumbing installation when a valve or fixture needs replacing.

Reading the toilet before you buy parts

Look inside the tank first. If the tank has a tall plastic tower in the middle instead of a flapper, it has a canister fill and flush assembly. Read the brand and model stamped inside the tank or under the lid. Photograph the old flapper next to a ruler.

Buy a flapper with the same number of connection points. Some flappers have two ears, others a rubber ring, and a few have an adjustable float arm. A universal flapper is fine if the opening size matches, but a brand-specific part usually fits better.

Parts standards and water-use guidance

Flappers are not universal. The standard sizes are 2 inch and 3 inch, and the right one depends on the flush valve opening in your tank, so measure before you buy. A mismatched flapper is the most common reason a new one still leaks.

According to the EPA WaterSense program, a labeled toilet uses no more than 1.28 gallons per flush, so a silent leak from a worn flapper can waste far more water than the toilet was designed to use.
