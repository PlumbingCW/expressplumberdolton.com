Almost every toilet problem is one of three things. A tank part has worn out, the seal under the bowl has failed, or the drain behind the bowl is blocked. They need different fixes, so a leaking toilet and a clogged toilet should never be treated the same way.

Toilet overflowing right now: what to do while you wait

Water on a bathroom floor travels fast, especially into the ceiling below. These steps take under a minute and need no tools.

Close the supply valve

It sits on the wall or floor behind the bowl. Turn it clockwise. If it is stuck or snaps, shut the main water valve instead and tell us.

Stop the tank from refilling

Lift the lid and hold the float up, or press the rubber flapper down over the drain opening. The bowl cannot overflow if the tank stops adding water.

Do not flush again

A second flush on a blocked drain just sends more water over the rim. Wait until we confirm what is blocked.

Contain and protect

Lay towels at the base, move rugs, and keep power cords and outlets clear of wet floor. If the water came from the bowl, treat it as dirty and wear gloves.

Check the room below

A stain or drip on the ceiling under the bathroom means water is moving through the floor. Tell the dispatcher so we plan for it.

A running toilet usually means one of five parts

A toilet that hisses, refills on its own, or needs the handle jiggled is wasting water around the clock. The cause is normally a worn part, and one visit usually settles it.

Flapper

The rubber disc that seals the tank. When it warps or collects mineral crust, tank water leaks into the bowl and the fill valve restarts every few minutes.

Fill valve

The tall assembly that refills the tank. A worn seal inside keeps it hissing or lets water creep past the shutoff point.

Float height

If the float sits too high, water spills down the overflow tube. Lowering it is sometimes the only fix needed.

Chain or lift arm

Too much slack under the flapper chain prevents a clean seal. Too little holds the flapper open.

Overflow tube

A cracked tube or a refill hose clipped at the wrong angle drains the tank slowly and goes unnoticed.

Water around the base: the wax ring and the flange

A toilet sits on a wax ring pressed between the bowl horn and the floor flange. When that ring is crushed or the toilet has been allowed to rock, a thin film of water seeps out at the base on every flush. Floors under vinyl and tile can rot for months before anyone sees a stain.

Pulling the toilet to replace the ring is also our chance to inspect the flange. A cracked or corroded flange, or one sitting below the finished floor, cannot hold a new ring tight. We tell you what we see before we reset the bowl, and a flange repair ring or a new flange is a separate, clearly quoted step.

A toilet that moves when you sit on it is a warning. Loose closet bolts or a failing seal are easy to fix early and costly to fix once the subfloor is soft.

Fix the toilet you have or replace it?

Repairs make sense far more often than people expect, but not every toilet is worth the parts.

Repair it when

  • The bowl and tank are sound and the problem is a flapper, fill valve or wax ring
  • The flush works well and only the refill is wrong
  • You like the height and look of the toilet
  • The toilet has not needed repeated service visits

Replace it when

  • The porcelain is cracked at the tank, bowl or base
  • It clogs often even with a clear drain line
  • Internal parts are no longer made for that model
  • You want a taller bowl or a toilet that flushes with less water

A cracked tank or bowl is never repaired with epoxy, because the crack will open again under load. If you decide to swap, our plumbing installation crew handles the supply line, shutoff valve and floor fit in the same visit.

How a replacement toilet goes in

Whether you bought the toilet or want help choosing, the sequence is the same.

1

Shut off and empty

We close the supply, flush the tank, and sponge the bowl dry so the toilet lifts cleanly.

2

Pull the old toilet and inspect

The flange, closet bolts and the floor around them are checked for cracks, rust or soft spots.

3

Set the new ring and bowl

We confirm the distance from the wall to the flange bolts, since 12 inches is common but not universal, then set the bowl level and snug the bolts evenly.

4

Connect and test

We attach a new supply line, flush several times, and watch the base and the supply joint for seepage.
