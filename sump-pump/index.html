A sump pump sits in a pit in the lowest part of the basement and pushes groundwater outside before it reaches the floor. When it quits, there is no second line of defense unless you have a battery backup. That is why a stuck switch on a stormy night turns into a real problem so quickly.

Sump pump failed in a storm: what to do first

Water and electricity do not mix. Your safety comes before the carpet.

Do not wade in

If water is near outlets, a furnace or the electrical panel, stay upstairs. Call the 24/7 line and, if you must, your electric utility.

Check the power path

Look at the plug, the GFCI button and the breaker. A tripped GFCI is the most common reason a sump pump goes silent.

Lift the float

If you can see the pit and it is safe, raise the float by hand. If the pump starts and the water level drops, the float or its switch is the problem.

Buy time with a bucket or wet vac

Bail water from the pit if it is reachable and dry ground allows. Do not run a wet vac in standing water near outlets.

Move valuables and take photos

Lift boxes off the floor and photograph the water, the pit and the damage. You will need pictures for any insurance claim.

Why Dolton basements flood during storms

Cook County's hazard mitigation plan for Dolton describes repeated basement flooding across whole neighborhoods, naming the Cornell area and the 158th and Greenwood corridor among others. If you live near those areas, a working sump pump and a backup plan are not extras. Test the pump every season, keep a backup power source, and do not wait for the water. The plan also notes that some flooding comes from sewer backup, which a sump pump cannot stop.

Five ways a sump pump lets you down

Most failures are not the motor. They are cheap parts or conditions around it.

Stuck float switch

The float jams against the pit wall or the discharge pipe and never signals the pump to start, or never signals it to stop.

Failed check valve

The valve on the discharge line holds water above the pump. If it fails, water falls back into the pit and the pump cycles constantly.

Clogged intake

Silt, gravel and debris in the pit block the intake screen and the pump overheats or loses flow.

Burned-out motor

A pump that runs without moving water, or runs for years without service, eventually stops.

Blocked discharge line

A crushed, kinked or clogged outside pipe stops the water from leaving, even though the pump runs.

Battery backup: what it can and cannot do

A backup pump is the single best addition for a flood-prone basement, but it has limits.

What it does

  • Runs on a 12 volt battery when the power goes out
  • Starts automatically if the main pump fails or the water rises too high
  • Often includes an alarm so you know the main pump has stopped
  • Buys hours of protection during a storm outage

What it cannot do

  • Pump at the same rate as a full-size main pump during heavy inflow
  • Run forever, since the battery drains and wears out
  • Stop sewage from coming up a floor drain
  • Replace regular testing and maintenance

The discharge line: where the water goes

A pump that runs but does not clear the pit often has a discharge problem. The pipe may be crushed, packed with debris, frozen solid, or pointed so the water runs back toward the foundation.

We trace the line from the pit to the outside and check where it ends. If the line has to be dug up, Illinois law requires a call to JULIE (811) at least 48 hours before anyone digs, so utility lines are marked first.

Does your insurance cover a sump pump failure?

The Illinois Department of Insurance says basic homeowners insurance does not cover floods, and that standard flood coverage does not typically cover damage from sewer backups or sump pump problems. It advises asking your agent about adding sewer backup or sump pump coverage. The consumer line is 1-866-445-5364.

Read your policy before a storm, not after. If you plan to buy NFIP flood coverage at floodsmart.gov, it takes effect 30 days after purchase, so buying during a storm will not help. After any loss, photograph the damage and make an inventory of what was affected.

Pit cleaning and seasonal testing

A little maintenance prevents a lot of storm-night phone calls.

  • Pour a bucket of water into the pit and confirm the pump starts and shuts off
  • Unplug the pump and lift it out only if you know the pit is clean and safe to work in
  • Remove sediment and gravel from the bottom of the pit
  • Check that the float moves freely without rubbing the pit wall
  • Look at where the discharge pipe ends outside and clear it if blocked
  • Test the battery backup and replace the battery on schedule
